# Supplementary Notes for the Offshore Wind and Surf-Tourism Game Model

This file preserves supporting material that was removed from the article appendix. It is intended for the GitHub artifact, not for the journal manuscript.

## Compensatory Measures in Portugal

The Portuguese legal setting treats compensatory measures as more than direct cash transfers. Decree-Law No. 140/99 frames compensation in relation to the ecological and territorial effects of a project, especially where impacts cannot be fully avoided and protected habitats or uses are affected.

Sources used in the article:

- Diario da Republica, Decree-Law No. 140/99: <https://diariodarepublica.pt/dr/detalhe/decreto-lei/140-1999-531828>
- ICNF guidance on compensatory measures: <https://www.icnf.pt/api/file/doc/3401bfd118136d67>

For the surf-tourism case, this creates an evidentiary problem. If the relevant loss concerns wave quality, submerged morphology, event viability, or destination attractiveness, the affected side must translate those changes into evidence that can be recognized in licensing and negotiation processes.

## Ericeira and Surf-Tourism Value

The Ericeira support material reinforces the idea that surf value is broader than direct sporting expenditure. Surfing supports restaurants, bars, surf camps, surf houses, specialized accommodation, food and beverage consumption, and transport demand.

This material supports the article's treatment of surf-related value as a local economic web rather than a narrow recreational activity.

## Questions Raised by the Peniche Notes

The older Peniche notes framed the dispute as a tension between two public-policy objectives:

- renewable-energy expansion, justified by decarbonization and the energy transition;
- local economic development tied to surf-driven tourism, place promotion, and community livelihood.

The notes also emphasized that environmental assessment should not rely only on an "average wave" if the economic resource is a specific surfable wave profile. The key question is whether the altered wave remains suitable for the same practice, users, events, and destination identity.

A further institutional question concerns compensation recipients. The surfing field may include local associations, event structures, informal service networks, surf schools, accommodation operators, and broader tourism actors. Their dependence on surfing may be real even when their bargaining representation is fragmented.

Open audit status:

- The `I=22` benchmark is supported by the WSL/ISEG economic-impact material.
- The exact recipient of compensation remains a modelling and institutional-design question, not a settled empirical fact.
- The Player 2 coalition that combines local tourism representation and WSL still requires author confirmation.

## Game-Theory Background

The conflict studied in the article is related to, but not identical with, the tragedy of the commons. In commons problems, individually rational decisions may degrade a shared resource even when all actors would benefit from restraint. In this article, the structure is narrower: one coalition advances a project and another resists it.

The distinction between Nash equilibrium and Pareto superiority remains central. A strategic outcome can be stable because no player wants to deviate unilaterally and still be socially worse than another available outcome.

Policy intervention does not need to rely only on punishment. In game-theory terms, strategic outcomes can shift by reducing the payoff from non-cooperation, increasing the payoff from cooperation, or changing the cost structure that sustains conflict.

## Model Caveats

The current model is static, aggregates heterogeneous actors into two coalitions, and treats the dispute as a one-shot decision. Real maritime conflicts can unfold sequentially, repeat across licensing stages, and include reputation effects, coalition fragmentation, and learning over time.

Uncertainty is represented in reduced form through a single probability of offshore success. Litigation costs are also represented as direct parameters rather than as alternative legal-cost regimes with different cost-shifting rules.

The model should therefore be read as a strategic map of parameter effects, not as a full institutional or hydrodynamic simulation.
